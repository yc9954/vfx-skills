# Load, balance and handling

Use for lifting, carrying, pouring, pushing, pulling, stance and locomotion. Treat
the principles as production hypotheses to make effort visible, not as a worker-
safety calculation or proof of internal forces.

## Support and balance model

- Name the actual base of support: both feet, one foot, feet plus a hand, seat,
  moving platform, or another explicit support. A root control is not a support.
- A wider or reoriented stance and a lower body position can increase static
  stability. Dynamic gait, turning and flight cannot be rejected by a static
  “CoM inside feet” rule.
- Root/pelvis is not whole-body center of mass. With no segment mass model, use a
  stated CoM proxy only to compare poses; do not call it measured CoM, CoP or force.
- The body and prop form a combined system. When the prop moves away from the body,
  its load line and moment arm usually require more visible counterbalance or effort.

## Moment arm and load distance

For animation, the practical relation is qualitative: the same prop held farther
from the body usually demands more joint torque and clearer whole-body response.
Therefore inspect not only prop weight but also hand distance, height, asymmetry,
grip quality, duration and whether the character is accelerating, stopping or
precisely positioning it. Do not invent kilograms or torque values when the scene
has no calibrated mass/force model.

Visible consequences may include closer load carriage, elbows staying flexed,
stance change, slower acceleration, trunk counter-lean, foot pressure/step changes,
brief corrective adjustments and a more deliberate settle. Do not stack all cues
automatically; choose the ones the shot can show.

## Anticipatory postural adjustment

A voluntary limb or prop movement can disturb posture. Before the focal action,
show the smallest readable preparation appropriate to its scale and speed:

- gaze or head identifies the target;
- feet establish a usable stance or one foot prepares to accept load;
- pelvis/trunk shifts before the arms take the prop;
- shoulders and elbows organize the approach;
- fingers pre-shape before contact.

Preparation must precede the disturbance it explains. A torso leaning only after
the jug is already airborne is a catch-up reaction, not anticipation.

## Two-handed lifting and carrying

1. Confirm the prop is initially supported.
2. Approach with enough space for both hands and elbows.
3. Pre-shape each hand for its role: load-bearing, stabilizing, steering or tipping.
4. Establish distributed contact and grip before support transfer.
5. Shift body weight and begin lift without an abrupt prop pop.
6. Keep the prop reasonably close unless the story requires extension; if it moves
   outward, add corresponding balance/effort and preserve wrist/shoulder plausibility.
7. During carry, coordinate steps with the held load; remove unrelated free-arm swing.
8. Establish the destination support before reducing grip and withdrawing the hands.

Two hands are not automatically safer or more plausible. Their spacing, different
roles, object geometry and shared load path must be visible. A hand touching the
vessel wall without supporting or controlling it is not a verified second grip.

## Pushing and pulling

- Identify the resistance, force direction, contact height, hand spacing, footing,
  friction assumption and travel path.
- Prefer whole-body contribution when the task reads as substantial: stance and
  legs/trunk organize before the arms, then the body travels with the load.
- Pushing often allows the body to remain behind the load and use forward weight
  transfer; pulling can require backward/sideways travel and clear sight/foot space.
  Do not universalize either without the current geometry.
- Separate breakaway effort, sustained movement, steering, stopping and release.
  A cart or heavy lid should not maintain speed after the body stops driving it
  unless inertia or another cause is part of the scene.

## Locomotion and stance under load

- Before a step, weight transfers to the support side; the swing foot clears, lands
  and accepts load before the other foot releases.
- Turning with a load requires a pivot or replant. Avoid twisting through two fully
  planted feet while the torso and prop rotate far away.
- The held object's inertia affects starts, stops and turns. Check prop lag, grip
  stability and whether the torso/feet react, while avoiding floaty secondary motion.
- Preserve deliberate side steps, backward steps, dance, disability-specific gait
  and assistive supports. “Face the travel direction” is not a universal fix.

## Follow-through and settling

After the primary action, decelerate the chain and resolve support:

- the prop stops without an unexplained snap or drift;
- hands make small stabilizing corrections only if motivated;
- shoulders/trunk/pelvis return from effort with staggered timing;
- feet remain or replant into a stable end state;
- after placing, the new support takes the load before grip releases;
- liquid/cloth/secondary motion may continue according to its own specialist contract.

Follow-through is not generic wobble. It should reveal residual momentum, load or
recovery and then settle into a state from which the next action can begin.

## Miniature-scale readability

Small projected characters lose subtle contact and weight cues. Preserve truth,
then improve readability by using a clean silhouette, visible hand separation,
clear support foot, phase spacing and short holds at grip acquisition, maximum tilt
or end support. Slightly enlarge timing contrast before enlarging joint excursions.
Do not scale a real-world degree table, add cartoon recoil by default or claim that
human-size motion obeys physically scaled miniature dynamics.
