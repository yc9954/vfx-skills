# Overlap, follow-through and secondary action

Layer motion after the primary idea and causal chain work. Overlap is not general
softness; it is a specific order in which connected masses, controls or thoughts
begin, continue and stop.

## Lead, drag, follow and settle

For each layered element record:

`driver | follower | start lag | path/drag | stop lag | settle character | fixed contact`

Examples:

- torso turns; shoulder follows; upper arm, forearm and hand successively redirect;
- forearm stops the jug; fingers maintain pressure; sleeve continues briefly and
  folds back toward the wrist;
- eyes acquire a target; head follows; ribcage follows only if the task needs it;
- body lands; soft tissue and costume absorb after the skeleton, without moving feet.

Use lag relative to scale, material, stiffness, speed and intention. A rigid tool,
loaded hand, loose sleeve and nervous finger do not share one delay value.

## Successive breaking of joints

Successive action lets a chain articulate in sequence instead of rotating as one
board. It is most visible when direction changes or energy travels through the body.

- Determine where force or intent begins.
- Let proximal and distal segments respond in a plausible sequence.
- Preserve the working-range and contact constraints from biomechanics.
- Reverse or compress the order when the character deliberately leads with a hand,
  eye or tool; the phrase describes coordination, not an invariant formula.
- Check for rubber-hose delay that makes a loaded arm look disconnected.

## Gesture hierarchy

- **Primary**: the action or pose that carries the scene objective.
- **Secondary**: an action that reinforces character, emotion or comprehension while
  preserving the primary read.
- **Tertiary**: small detail—fingers, breath, cheek, cloth, jewelry, hair—that adds
  texture after the first two layers are stable.

At any instant ask what should be read first. Reduce amplitude, delay or frequency
of lower layers if they compete. Secondary action should not create a new objective
unless the story intentionally changes.

## Follow-through and recovery

Follow-through completes energy after the main driver changes or stops. Recovery
returns the character to a usable next state. They may overlap but are not identical.

- Prop contact remains continuous while the hand owns the load.
- The follower can overshoot or lag, but cannot detach, penetrate or reverse ownership.
- Use staggered arrival: prop/arms, torso/shoulders, sleeves/fingers, breath/face may
  resolve on nearby but distinct beats.
- End with a clear support, velocity and attention target. “Still moving a little”
  is not a usable continuation state.

## Breathing and idle motion

Breath supports phrasing and effort. It may compress before a lift, suspend during
precision, release after success or remain almost invisible for a guarded character.
Do not scale the whole torso uniformly or run a perpetual sine loop.

Idle is active attention. Keep the key pose and support stable while using sparse
breath, eye refocus, finger pressure, cloth decay or a small balance correction.
The pattern should respond to new events instead of continuing through them unchanged.

## Simulation boundary

Animation specifies intention, protected shapes and timing windows. Cloth/hair/soft
tissue simulation may generate detail, but it must preserve silhouette, face, grip,
camera and acting accents. Cache success is not performance approval. If simulation
smears an important pose or creates perpetual motion, art-direct or reduce it.

## Failure patterns

- every control offset by the same two frames;
- all followers oscillate with the same frequency and damping;
- sleeve drag pulls the wrist off the handle;
- secondary hand gesture contradicts effort or attention;
- facial bounce ignores head/body dynamics;
- moving holds become random noise;
- tertiary motion starts before the primary cause.
